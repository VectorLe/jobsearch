The Transcendental Argument

"I think, therefore I am." This is the famous "cogito, ergo sum" posited by René Descartes in the 17th century. He believed that it was the most foundational, most undoubtable statement one could rely on which all other statements about reality would be built upon.

However, what seems to be the "most fundamental belief" comes crumbling down when zooming out. Descartes makes a number of ontological assumptions upon which he builds this statement that he claims to be foundational to all other statements that can be made. Let's dive deeper into his claim.

  • "I think": This is an assumption that the mind making the statement is one that holds a single identity indefinitely. The conscious mind cannot be operating out of a multiplicity of identities, which is inherently learned through experience. We as humans have never experienced thinking through the consciousness of another person's identity. Descartes makes a major assumption in believing that thought can only be formed through the first person, and this is learned through experience. Can not our experience deceive us? How then can we say that this is absolutely ontologically true? Do we only know "I think" through our own experience?
  • "... therefore": This is an assumption that there is an ontological unchanging law of logic that allows statements to make sense through deduction. X, therefore Y is a logical statement that can only exist if there also exists a system through which the world is governed, that is, through logic. The statement also assumes that if such a law of logic exists, that it is not subject to changing at any given time. Logic is a system that must therefore exist as an unchanging natural order that grounds all things in the universe. This is ultimately a major ontological claim that Descartes makes through the invocation of the word "therefore".

Perhaps the more foundational statement could be, "There is", just implying being and nothing else. Beyond all these major metaphysical assumptions, it is also doubtful that Descartes realizes that through making statements about reality that he is also assuming even more metaphysical claims. That is,

  • His thoughts can be expressed through language
  • Other minds exist through which the meaning of his statement can be transmitted to
  • An ontological reality exists such that his statement could be true as opposed to false
  • If another mind can understand the meaning behind his statement then they ought to believe what is true as opposed to what is false
  • If truth exists, that it not only does not change, but can also be derived through human rationality

Fundamentally foundationalism, and especially the kind of foundationalism posited by Descartes, cannot stand as a justified belief system. In order to have a justified belief system, there must be some deeper, more fundamental ground layer upon which statements can even be allowed to exist.

What then, can we actually know? This is the central question of epistemology. Cutting into the root of Descartes' various assumptions, we are left with more questions than answers: Can Descartes' assumptions be accounted for? And if so, are there any further assumptions we make in justifying them? What truly is the underlying structure of reality?

To answer these questions, let's take a look at the transcendental argument.

Transcendental categories exist, therefore God exists.

Transcendental Categories - categories that transcend the observable material world (e.g. matter, etc). These are metaphysical entities that exist but cannot be observed through empirical evidence:

Logic

  • Logic is a system through which true or false statements can be made
  • A=A, A != !A, numbers, arithmetics
  • Under the rules of logic, there are logical fallacies which can be made (i.e. circular reasoning). Logical fallacies are statements which cannot be deemed as true/justified since they are invalid within the system of logic.
  • Within the system of logic, logic itself cannot be proven
  • "Logic, therefore logic" is not a valid logical statement. Logic cannot be self-evident.
  • Since logic exists but cannot be proven within the system of logic, it must necessarily follow that there is a higher system that allows for logic to exist.
  • Science for example derives its justification in physics. Physics derives its justification from math. Math derives its justification from logic.
  • Logic is self-evident and justifies itself?
    • No, this is circular reasoning.

Identity Over Time

  • A=A in all cases, indefinitely
  • This statement cannot hold true if there is no underlying system to uphold it. It could not be the case in a universe where all things are random, since this is not random and instead holds across all cases.
  • In a completely random universe free of logic, A = A sometimes, and sometimes A = B even when B = !A. There is no underlying structure for which A must equal A in all possible conditions.

Consciousness

  • Consciousness is experienced in the first person, and is the only means through which we can have experiences
  • We as conscious beings have experiences within our mind (i.e. personal thoughts, the act of learning, etc)
  • We as conscious beings also have experiences within our physical body
  • We cannot have the experience of being another person, therefore consciousness is bound to identity
  • Consciousness interacts with the material world but is not in itself material
  • It cannot be an emergent property of neurons constructed in a particular pattern since the immaterial cannot be generated from the material.
  • It interacts with the material world through physical bodies. Bodies are constrained to physical laws and sensory input.
  • When a physical body interacts with physical stimuli such as physical pain/weariness, mind-altering substances, or death, the consciousness tied to that physical body also experiences changes.
  • Chinese Room Thought Experiment:
    • Suppose there are two rooms with people who speak different languages and have no means of communicating with each other except through writing and sending physical messages. Inside each room is a dictionary containing rational responses to every permutation of the other person's language. If each person copied the text in the dictionary perfectly to send messages to one another, the other person would be under the illusion that the person they are writing to understand the meaning behind what they are saying. Although they would have a full and meaningful conversation from each other's perspectives, the actual meaning is not being conveyed from the messages to their brains. In this case, there is no conscious perception of meaning, therefore consciousness must transcend syntax.

Ethics

  • Ethics are a means through which we make value judgements
  • We "ought" to believe what is true, as opposed to what is false
  • We "ought" to have debate because we believe our perspectives have more value over another's, and therefore they should be informed of our perspective

Knowledge

  • Knowledge for humans is the storage of interpretations of truth via experience or communication.
  • Knowledge cannot exist without a conscious mind to store it.
  • Knowledge of complex ideas that exist such as numbers, advanced mathematics, the concept of infinity, the Mandelbrot Set show that we as humans are discovering knowledge that already exists.
  • If the knowledge is being discovered and knowledge is only stored in a conscious mind, there must be a conscious mind that has stored this knowledge prior to our discovery of it.

Causality

  • Causality is the the relationship between cause and effect, given that time is linear
  • All temporal events that occur have a causal event that occurred prior to provide the conditions for the present event

These are all principles which are, even under a secular worldview:

  • Unchanging
  • Require no observational evidence
  • Necessary preconditions for knowledge, thought, experience, and being

When you start a convsersation, the necessary assumptions are that these all exist.

  • Logic is valid. You expect the other person to be able to deduce valid logic and reasoning, while also rejecting logical fallacies
  • Truth exists. You believe that there is something to be discovered about reality
  • Truth is unchanging. You believe that if truth is found, that all the work done to arrive at that truth wasn't in vain because it doesn't change
  • Truth has value. You believe that truth should be adhered to as opposed to falsehood
  • Another conscious being exists. You believe that your conversation is with another being that can perceive knowledge

The interesting thing here to note is that the secular worldview agrees that debate can occur, but never accounts for why it's actually possible. Even the most brash of so-called skeptics seem to believe in transcendental categories as just "givens" when they have no grounds by which to justify them. How is it possible that all these various metaphysical entities exist, but are immaterial, unobservable, and coexist? How is it possible that human rationality not only uses these categories but can also philosophize about their nature? We therefore see that the materialist worldview cannot be upheld.

To put it another way, would logic, numbers, ethics, and so on exist without the event of "the big bang"? If so, what is it about the big bang that would allow these things to exist if not materially? If not, did they exist prior to the creation of all things? What is the origin of these immaterial, invariant concepts/laws? One could hold to the Platonic view that these entities all existed in the Monad, but that would beg the question of what the nature of the Monad is and also how distinctions could be made in the One (in the philosophical question of the One and the Many).

The existence of all transcendental categories can only be made sense of through God, a being that exists beyond all human understanding that can be the basis through which invariant, unchanging laws can exist. Without the concept of God, there is no other justified worldview that can explain them.

In "the beginning", all transcendental categories would have necessarily emerged at the same "time" or exist outside of time altogether. Each specific transcendental could not have existed independently. If knowledge exists, knowledge of what? If ethics exist, by what means can it be exhibited? If logic exists, what would a logical operation be? And so on.

Circling back to the central question of epistemology, how then can we truly know anything? The answer is through divine revelation. We can only truly know what has been revealed to us by the invariant, all-knowing, unchanging creator of reality, God. The mind of God is the source of truth, the grounding through which transcendental categories can exist, and whose image and likeness that we as humans bear in order to both use and reflect on the nature of these transcendental categories. For what purpose would humans have the ability to philosophize about such abstract concepts about the underlying structure of the universe that bear no immediately practical implications in secular evolutionary terms?
