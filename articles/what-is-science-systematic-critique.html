What is Science? A Systematic Critique

The modern secular worldview regards science as the primary means through which we obtain knowledge. After all, it gave us all the wonders of technology that we have today, and we know much more about the world now than ever before than in the times when "we didn't have science".

But is this worldview correct? What even is science? Can it really give us knowledge about ontological reality? What gives it a unique distinction such that we can grant it such authority to tell us about reality?

The Demarcation Problem

The demarcation problem is a long-standing problem in the philosophy of science that attempts to ask exactly that:

"What exactly constitutes 'science'? What is the line that distinguishes 'science' from 'non-science'?"

You might think something along the lines of, "That's easy. With all the advancements today, we know exactly what science is, and it's simply just what we know from the scientific method."

So, is the defining feature that makes science what it is just the method? Observe, hypothesize, test, conclude? Seems reasonable, but how reliable is that view?

Could it be the case that your observations deceive you? Or that the observation method itself changes the conditions of the event being observed? Can every possible condition of an event be accounted for? Could every single variable be accounted for? Could it be the case that you test a hypothesis and the results are always the same but the underlying hypothesis posits an incorrect view of ontological reality? Can science even make statements about ontological reality, or is it just a system through which we make practical views of the observable world in a strictly material sense?

Maybe this question wasn't so simple after all. Let's take a deeper dive into how some of the great minds throughout the ages have tackled this issue.

Karl Popper

Where Karl Popper draws the line of demarcation between science and non-science is falsifiability. Essentially, he posits that hypotheses made must in principle be falsifiable, that is, able to be proven wrong through empirical data.

Generally, Popper's view is that science's main goal is to falsify, not verify. Each successive observational discovery should be used to see if existing hypotheses could be falsified. If a hypothesis withstands repeated attempts at falsification, then the hypothesis can be considered tentatively valid until proven otherwise and if not, then the hypothesis can be rejected. No hypothesis can ever truly be verified but only tentatively accepted until proven otherwise.

Additionally, this view lends itself towards separating what the modern education system sees as the hard sciences (physics, chemistry, etc) vs the soft sciences (psychology, sociology, etc). Although both may use the scientific method, only the hard sciences have falsifiable hypotheses.

This was Popper's view on the demarcation problem. Problem solved, right? Where exactly does this view fall short? It seems pretty rock solid.

Well, is that how science actually works? We just look for evidence to falsify theories, and change our views based on new evidence? Don't we often have wide acceptance of theories even though they have unexplained anomalies? Take for instance the following anomalies modern science has built up:

  • Dark matter & dark energy supposedly accounts for 95% of the universe but can't be detected through any means of observation. For all the science we've done and all that we've figured out about the universe, 95% is not just a minor anomaly, but perhaps something that shows we're on the completely wrong path in our approach.
  • The Fermi paradox brings forth a probabilistic mystery which basically asks, "Where is everybody?" Given all the amount of time that has elapsed since the supposed inception of the universe during the Big Bang and all the various star systems, the probability of human life being the only life in the entire universe seems highly unlikely.
  • The conditions for the origin of life (abiogenesis) on Earth are unknown, but thought to be such that life can proceed from non-life given enough time and some very specific conditions. This has never been reproduced under any lab conditions.
  • Göbekli Tepe is a major megalithic structure supposedly dated prior to the advent of agriculture. Conventional views of human history cannot account for why such a complex structure could have been built without agriculture or permanant settlements. The typical view is that agriculture enables a food surplus, allowing for specialization and social hierarchy, which then leads to urbanization, the development or religion, and then dedicated planning & labor for religious structures especially at the scale of Göbekli Tepe.
  • UFOs/UAPs seem to be well documented phenomena confirmed by U.S. intelligence and defense officials. These are supposedly objects that appear in the air and move in ways that completely defy what we "know" about the laws of physics and propulsion.
  • The Great Pyramids of Giza are a deep, deep rabbithole that I won't even get into here.

Now you might look at these anomalies and think, "Well yea they're anomalies but if we just keep looking for more evidence and get new information, we'll eventually figure it out and tweak our current theories and views about the world to account for them." In Popper's view, all such anomalies could be major red flags that show that existing theories may need to be radically revised.

While Popper's view may sound good in theory, what happens in reality is that anomalies are typically swept under the rug or it just gets accepted that they haven't explained by existing theories yet. Is it possible though that existing theories are not even close to describing reality? What if these anomalies just build up to the extent that existing theories can no longer stand as consensus? Thomas Kuhn provides interesting insights into this phenomenon.

Thomas Kuhn

Thomas Kuhn's approach to the demarcation problem is radically different from that of Popper's. His primary concern was not in the methods or components of the methods of science, but in the paradigmatic views that it gives rise to. He posits that science doesn't progress linearly through the gradual collection of new information and observations, but rather that science goes through paradigm shifts.

Essentially in Kuhn's view, all scientific observational data is viewed through the lense of paradigms and theories/worldviews eventually run into some degree of anomalies. This is necessarily the case, because not all variables and conditions of any observations can be accounted for. This is famously described by David Hume in his problem of induction which points out that just because something has behaved a certain way in the past, it does not mean that it must necessarily behave that way in the future.

Nassim Taleb illustrates this point perfectly in his example of "the turkey problem". Suppose a turkey scientist on a farm makes observations and collects data about its surroundings everyday. It observes that every morning, a farmer appears, and proceeds to feed the turkey scientist and all his turkey friends with an abundance of food. The turkey scientist collects this data for quite some time and forms a hypothesis: farmers love turkeys. The turkey scientist continues to observe this pattern without fail for months on end and is increasingly convinced of his hypothesis. There has never been a case in which his hypothesis has been falsified. However one cold, fateful day, on the last week of November, the inevitable happens. It's Thanksgiving. There was no data that the turkey could have collected to have predicted such an event occuring. In fact, his entire paradigm was incorrect, despite all the data.

Now circling back to Kuhn, we can observe that because we can never truly know all the variables/conditions of any given observation, our paradigms regarding observations and their causes & effects naturally pose anomalies. Not because the paradigms are necessarily wrong, but that we can never say with certainty anything is truly the case since we're always limited in what we can observe. Now, as a paradigm is widely accepted as "consensus" among scientists, these anomalies begin to accumulate. While science as it is can proceed with these anomalies, since we can always defer to the excuse "well, we'll just keep working on this path as we always have and eventually we'll find out the solution to these anomalies", eventually this excuse can no longer stand. The bubble bursts. A tipping point is eventually reached as the anomalies pile up, and an entire paradigm collapses.

What takes its place is a new paradigm. Not because it's necessarily correct or more certain than the old paradigm, but because it can better account for the old paradigm's anomalies, as a tradeoff for a few new ones. One of the biggest examples of this is your mother. The second biggest example is Einstein's general relativity vs Newtonian mechanics.

Case Study: Gravity

So in high school physics, we learned that gravity was a force so we apply Newton's second law: F=ma. We find that on Earth, our acceleration is "g", or -9.8 m/s² . We therefore use F=mg, and we find that the -9.8 m/s² number is actually derived from F=GMm/r² where GM/r² is g. G being a gravitational constant, M being the mass of the Earth, and r being the radius of Earth. If you're following, so far we have:

  • F=ma, where F=Force, m=mass, a=acceleration
  • F=mg, where g=acceleration from gravity on Earth, -9.8 m/s²
  • F=GMm/r², where G=gravitational constant, M/m=masses of 2 objects respectively, r=distance between the center of masses of the 2 objects. This is where F=mg comes from: because effectively on Earth, one of the objects will be Earth, and the value of r is the radius of the Earth.

This is Newton's "universal law of gravitation". The use of these formulas are effective and practical for calculating how gravitation affects nearly every case on Earth (falling objects, projectiles, etc), and even between celestial bodies (planetary orbits, etc). Okay, great thanks for the high school physics lesson. So what? Well, all of that "universal law of gravitation" stuff is basically just garbage, in an ontological sense. Despite the practicality in calculating even the effects of gravitation between celestial bodies, it still had a number of anomalies that it couldn't account for such as the bending of light around the sun and pinpoint accurate timekeeping to the precision of arcseconds for GPS satellites. It effectively works, but is that what we're interested in? Maybe for practical purposes, but speaking on ontological truth, we cannot say that Newtonian gravity is "truly the case".

So in comes Einstein's general relativity. Despite all the practicality of Newton's idea of gravity, Einstein says gravity isn't even a force. He then goes on tostate that gravity is a curvature of spacetime, not a force that attracts two masses. Einstein's field equation, which I won't claim to know the details of, is something entirely different. However under the special conditions we have here on Earth, it essentially reduces down to Newton's equation. The thing is though with Einstein's equation, it also accounts for all the anomalies of Newton's equation such as the bending of light around the sun and precise timekeeping. So despite the effectiveness of Newton's "universal law of gravitation, it didn't actually tell us about what was actually the case. While Einstein's general relativity might give us a better picture of what's going on by essentially reducing Newton's gravitation to being a special case within Einstein's framework, it is still possible that it doesn't tell us about actual reality.

Wow with all that, can we even say that science is able to make ontological claims? Or is it just making practical claims and calling it "scientific truth" or "laws" that are effective in explaining "most conditions". In the sense of the wider universe, all the conditions we conduct experiments on and calculate could actually just all be special case scenarios. So we have two problems:

  1. Science cannot make claims about ontological reality
  2. Science can only make claims in a practical sense, and even within the practicality framework, it is only regarding special cases (let's say, claims about the third dimension, within this one observable universe out of a possible many, with instruments that humans made, providing data that humans can percieve, etc).

So are we getting closer to answering the problem of demarcation? Is it even a real question to think about? Is science... dare I say, even real?

Paul Feyerabend

Now, for the grand finale of the demarcation problem. Paul Feyerabend. What a guy. Feyerabend essentially takes the whole problem of demarcation, crumples it up, and throws in the garbage. "W-w-what? B-but what about science? How do we know what science is?," you might ask. Well, Feyerabend is a proponent of epistemological anarchism which is summed up in his famous phrase "anything goes". It basically posits that there is no difference in the methods between science and pseudoscience.

Wait so science is fake and gay? Yes! Get owned. He uses the example of Galileo, who's widely seen as a kind of "hero of science, fighting against the irrational religious zealots who knew absolutely nothing known as the Catholic Church". Now, was this really the case? Let's take a historical look at Galileo and his defense of Copernican heliocentrism.

Case Study: Galileo

So Galileo's hypothesis was that Earth rotates on an axis. The issue witht his hypothesis is that it actually introduced more anomalies than the prevailing paradigm of motion. Even worse, it wasn't actually through incremental observational data that he used to defend his position, but rather through what was basically just trolling.

He wrote a book called "Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems" which was a hypothetical conversation between a very smart guy (a stand-in for himself) and a dummy named "Simplicio" (basically a simpleton). They'd go back and forth comparing Copernican heliocentrism and Ptolemy's geocentrism, and it was kind of like a big meme. Even in his book, he makes a series of inconsistent arguments and is unable to justify anomalies like how his theory couldn't accurately account for how tides behave. Einstein's commentary on this book was:

"It was Galileo's longing for a mechanical proof of the motion of the earth which misled him into formulating a wrong theory of the tides."

Additionally, the idea that Earth rotated on an axis introduced anomalies he couldn't explain regarding the brightness of Mars and Venus, as well as for the displacement of objects when dropped from tall heights. These are only explained by later developments by Kepler's laws of planetary motion, the Coriolis effect, and Newtonian mechanics (which we talked about earlier).

Along with the various anomalies, he famously introduces evidence observed through his telescope. Now, nobody in the world had such a weird contraption at this time. He was basically telling people, "Look, I made this magic device that lets me look at things super far away. I made it myself using some mirrors in a tube." He introduces a device which not only did he himself did not understand via Kepler's theory of optics, but also in his demonstration of the telescope to various astronomers in Padua, it produced double images, optical illusions, and afterimages. Now, that's not to say that his telescope was actually inaccurate, but rather that nobody had any logical, scientifically grounded reasons to believe that they were accurate. Effectively, this dude was literally just trolling and propagandizing. In a sense, flopping his way to success.

Now based on what we've seen so far, he has disregarded what we now call the "scientific method", Popperian falsificationism, as well as ignored Kuhn's idea that science adopts the paradigm with the least anomalies. However, we now see that despite breaking all "the rules", he's now viewed as a "hero of science" and his view is now, at least effectively, "correct". Feyerabend notes that the unconventional methods of Galileo were actually effectively what we can call "science". So science doesn't actually need a demarcation from non-science, but should actually embrace all points of view as if they have some degree of merit even when observational evidence and previaling paradigms say otherwise.

Science Can't Make Ontological Claims

Okay, there we go. Science can't make ontological claims. What we have are essentially effective models of the world, indefinitely subject to anomalies and the potentiality for paradigm shifts. These models are not even necessarily derived from a particular method, nor are they always the models with the least anomalies. Additionally, these models are only ever considered effectively valid within an ever-present special condition. Whoa.

Let's drive the point home even further with another example. What is an atom? I won't claim to know the technical details much, but my point can be demonstrated with brief summaries:

Case Study: The Atomic Model

  • Rutherford's Model: Atoms were mostly empty space with a central positively charged nucleus and had electrons orbiting it. The anomaly here was that electrons didn't just get collapse into the postively charged nucleus, as it would according to classical physics, but instead left so much empty space.
  • Bohr's Model: In order to account for the anomaly in Rutherford's model, Bohr's model proposed that electrons had fixed orbits around the nucleus, with each orbit corresponding to an energy level. This accounted for why atoms could remain stable despite the opposite charges of the particles, and was demonstrated using the example of hydrogen. However, it could not account for the behavior of atoms with multiple electrons.
  • Schrödinger's Model : The model that we hold today. It posits that the position of electrons can be represented by a probabalistic cloud around the nucleus, supported by quantum mechanics and some fancy wave function stuff that I don't truly understand.

The point of this example is primarily that despite the various Kuhnian paradigm shifts exhibited by each atomic model, we still can't say what an "atom" actually is, if it even truly exists. Even Schrödinger's model is essentially a mathematical abstraction due to its probabalistic nature. These are all useful models from which we can make calculations and make predictions with, but we can't even really truly say we "know" what an atom is.

On top of all that, proponents of string theory are now saying that atoms aren't even fundamental. That there's a deeper layer consisting of vibrating strings operating in multiple dimensions. What if those strings are made up of even further layers of real entities? And if we can't describe what an atom is, which is what everything physical consists of, how can we even truly accurately describe the nature of anything in reality?

Okay so... we can't "truly" describe reality in the truest sense, but science as it is can at least be pragmatic, right?

The Human Factors of Science

Institutional Science

Science is done by scientists. Pretty simple there. Scientists are human, with the exception of that one turkey mentioned earlier. Humans have personal agendas and practical concerns. Bills to pay, families to feed, careers to build, reputations to keep, social pressures, personal ideologies to uphold, etc. That's just the reality of it. As cool as science sounds in a vacuum being a pure, objective pursuit of truth (even just practical truth), in reality that's just not how it goes.

In order for science to be conducted, money has to come from somewhere. A lot of science is in fact conducted with the funding of corporations. Now, why would corporations fund science? For money. Duh. In order to further propagate their own interests, corporations use science to back up their specific brands/reputations or even to build an entire paradigm about a product they sell. Essentially, propaganda.

A big, widely known example of this is that in the 60s, the Sugar Research Foundation funded studies by Harvard University to downplay the role of sugar in heart disease. This research also played in a role in overemphasizing the role of fats in heart disease, a belief that is still commonly held to this day. These biased studies not only got published by a prestigious university under the peer-review system, but also played a role in shaping government issued dietary guidelines. And this wasn't just some evil conspiracy, but pretty much just an alignment of economic incentives between two parties. How often do you think this occurs today?

Now if something like that can get through the peer-review system, and other reviewers are just referencing what's already been passed, how much of a recursive loop can this kind of a situation make? And this is just one example. Imagine a whole system, an interconnected web of incentive alignment, personal agendas, people just trying to get by, etc. What would it take to be heterodox? You would be risking rejection from peer-review, funding for your research, and get ostracized from others in the field. How could someone like Galileo possibly emerge through institutional science? What if someone like him just wanted a tenured position at a local university?

Taboo Science

Now, there may be some scientists who aren't bound by incentives of money or something as silly as that. Some scientists are real. Absolute purists who only want to pursue truth and expand on the collection of knowledge that we as humans have been working on for thousands of years. Yes, there must be some who pursue the greater goal of true knowledge acquisition. The brave, curious minds who advance human knowledge without need for money nor fame. But what if what interests them is... illegal to research? Or what if social pressures aren't just reputationally costly in the sense of a professional career, but hold real consequences for their lives?

There are various fields of science which operate in the blurred lines of morality. Race. Gender. IQ. Genetic engineering. And so on. Scientists operating in these realms are understandably ostracized, but how can science claim to be a system that seeks knowledge when not every realm of knowledge is even permissible?

Are there any examples of reputable scientists who got ostracized for pursuing the forbidden limits of science? You'd be surprised. What kind of taboo could the likes of said reputable scientists have possibly touched on to be ostracized after even monumental contributions to science? What could the institutions have possibly done to people with such a powerful desire to seek knowledge?

Case Study: James Watson, Race, & IQ

Oh boy, you read that header didn't you? That's right, James Watson, the co-discoverer of the double helix structure of DNA and 1962 Nobel Prize winner, got cancelled due to his commentary about a link between race and IQ/behavior. While some people in the scientific community have come to his defense, his comments were understandably not without major criticism. Now, whether his claims are substantiated by evidence or not is not the primary area of focus for the purposes of this writing, but it is clearly demonstrated that science has boundaries which even those who are curious cannot explore, whether true or not.

Case Study: Charles Darwin & Race

Well this might not come as much of a surprise considering the nature of Darwin's work. However, we often hear of Darwin's most famous work being entitled "On the Origin of Species". What's wrong with that? Well, the work he published was actually titled "On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection, or the Preservation of Favoured Races in the Struggle for Life". No one wants to tell you that. As far as his actual views, they are not drastically radical by modern standards, but controversy is surely often swept under the rug or sidestepped in some way.

Whether or not Darwin actually believed in strict distinctions between human races remains somewhat muddy, considering the widespread censorship of information dispensed through online search engines, but based on direct quotes it seems that although he argued against slavery and polgenism, he did have some degree of recognition that distinctions did exist whether significant or not. His more controversial views were primarilly in his work "Descent of Man", where he distinguishes between "civilized" and "savage" races and makes inferences about how mental traits could be selected for in certain populations. In today's academic world, even these comments could be considered reputationally risky.

Case Study: He Jianqui & CRISPR Babies

In more recent times, the He Jiankui affair became a widely recognized incident in which a Chinese scientist successfully edited the genomes of human embryos. His research understandably received international criticism and Chinese authorities consequently not only suspended his research, but also sentenced him to 3 years in prison and fined him for 3 million yuan (over $400,000 USD). Clearly, there are some areas of science that are strictly forbidden not only through the ostracism of peers, but also by law.

Not Ontological... Nor Practical?

So where does this leave us?

Not only does science not have the ability to make ontological claims, but it isn't even always providing the most practical or effective view. In fact, it can't even claim to be in the fullest pursuit of knowledge at all.

As demonstrated systematically through the exploration of the demarcation problem, we find that there is an insurmountable wall of epistomology that "science" simply cannot climb. We can never truly know even a modicum of what ontological reality is with science. In fact, we end up realizing that nearly all questions of knowledge are outside the realm of what science completely.

Questions like "does God exist" or even something like "what is X" are actually essentially questions for the realm of philosophy. Furthermore, even as we look at science as a tool for creating pragmatic models of the universe based on observational data, this gets heavily muddied when looking into the origins of how scientific research is conducted within a system heavily influenced by human factors.

This isn't to say that science is completely useless, but to grant it the authority from which to speak on matters of "truth" would be to ignore its obvious limitations. Even if the world was purely materialistic, which I'd argue it's not due to the existence of invariant metaphysical entities, science remains highly flawed and cannot be justifiably viewed as the "end all be all" of knowledge acquisition.
